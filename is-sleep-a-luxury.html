Is Sleep a Luxury?

The science, economics and politics of a good night’s rest

Sleep is one of the few things that every human being needs, regardless of where they live, what they earn or what they believe. Yet the conditions under which we sleep can differ enormously.

Imagine two people going to bed at the same time, in the same city, on the same night. Both have set their alarms for eight hours later. Both have put away their phones. Both intend to get a good night’s rest.

One closes the windows of a temperature-controlled bedroom, insulated from the sounds of the city. The other leaves a window open because the room is too hot. Outside, vehicles pass intermittently. Somewhere nearby, a dog barks. A family member shifts in the same room.

Both have allocated eight hours to sleep. Neither necessarily has the same opportunity to experience it.

This distinction is largely absent from the everyday conversation about sleep. We talk about bedtime routines, screen time, meditation, mattresses and the sounds we should listen to. We are encouraged to measure, monitor and optimise our rest.

These conversations are valuable. But they often begin with an assumption worth examining: that the conditions necessary for good sleep are within our control.

What happens when they are not?

Section 1The individualisation of sleep

Sleep has become an increasingly deliberate consumer activity. There are devices that measure it, applications that interpret it, products designed to improve it and entire categories of content dedicated to helping us fall asleep.

We can purchase darkness in the form of blackout curtains, quiet in the form of acoustic insulation, comfort in the form of temperature-controlled mattresses and a more predictable sensory environment in the form of sound machines.

Hush itself operates within this ecosystem. We create music and soundscapes that people choose to accompany their work, relaxation and rest. This makes the distinction between what sound can do and what we merely hope it can do particularly important to us.

There is nothing inherently problematic about seeking to improve one’s sleeping environment. The ability to do so can be valuable.

The difficulty arises when sleep becomes understood primarily as a matter of individual behaviour. When it does, the explanation for inadequate sleep tends to reside with the individual: insufficient discipline, poor habits, excessive stimulation or a failure to prioritise rest.

But the ability to prioritise sleep is itself influenced by circumstances.

A person working unpredictable shifts cannot necessarily maintain a consistent bedtime. Someone sharing a crowded home may have little control over when the lights go out. A resident living beside a busy road cannot silence the traffic. A parent caring for an infant, or an adult caring for an unwell relative, cannot simply decide to remain undisturbed for eight hours.

Sleep is experienced individually. Many of its determinants are not.

Section 2Eight hours is not always eight hours

Sleep is often discussed in terms of duration. How many hours did you sleep last night? Did you get enough?

Duration is relatively intuitive and easy to communicate. But sleep is a multidimensional biological process.

A 2025 scientific statement from the American Heart Association identifies several dimensions of sleep health, including duration, continuity, timing, regularity, satisfaction and daytime functioning.1

1 St-Onge et al. 2025
Statement
American Heart Association scientific statement

There is also sleep architecture: the distribution and progression of different sleep stages throughout the night.

Two people who sleep for the same amount of time may experience different levels of fragmentation, different amounts of deep sleep and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, and different degrees of alertness the following day.

This complicates the way we think about sleep inequality.

If we measure access to sleep only by the number of hours available, we may overlook differences in what happens during those hours.

A bedroom can be occupied for eight hours without providing eight hours of sleep. And eight hours of sleep need not constitute the same physiological experience across different environments.

Section 3The sleeping brain is still listening

Sound provides a particularly interesting way to examine this distinction.

Unlike closing our eyes to light, we cannot voluntarily switch off our auditory system. The sleeping brain continues to process aspects of its acoustic environment, and sounds can disrupt sleep without necessarily producing awakenings that are remembered the following morning.

In 2026, sleep researchers investigated the effects of intermittent environmental noise and continuous pink noise in a controlled laboratory experiment involving 25 healthy adults across seven nights. Participants’ sleep was measured using polysomnography, enabling the researchers to examine sleep stages rather than relying solely on self-reported experiences.2

2 Basner et al. 2026
Lab experiment
25 healthy adults, 7 nights, polysomnography

Intermittent environmental noise reduced deep sleep relative to a noise-free control condition. Continuous pink noise, at the tested exposure level of 50 dBA when played alone, reduced REM sleep by approximately 19 minutes.

The latter finding is particularly interesting because continuous noise is widely used as a sleep aid.

An earlier systematic review, published in 2021, had already concluded that the evidence supporting continuous noise for improving sleep was of very low certainty.3

3 Riedy et al. 2021
Systematic review
38 studies; certainty of evidence rated very low

The 2026 experiment also examined earplugs as a means of mitigating environmental noise. Under the conditions studied, earplugs offered greater protection against several adverse effects of noise than pink noise.

These findings should not be interpreted as universal conclusions about sound machines, every type of continuous noise or every person experiencing sleep difficulties. The experiment involved a specific population, controlled exposures and particular sound levels.

Nevertheless, it reveals an important distinction.

Masking an unwanted sound introduces additional acoustic energy into the environment. Attenuating that sound reduces the energy reaching the listener. Both can alter the experience of noise, but they are not physiologically equivalent interventions.

This raises a question that extends beyond sleep science.

Why is the response to an undesirable environment so often another product purchased by the person experiencing it?

Section 4The geography of quiet

Noise is not distributed uniformly across a city.

Roads, railways, airports, construction sites, industrial facilities and entertainment districts create different acoustic environments. Exposure depends partly on proximity, building design, insulation and the activities taking place nearby.

The ability to avoid these environments is also unevenly distributed.

In 2017, researchers examined socioeconomic and racial disparities in estimated environmental noise exposure across the contiguous United States.4

4 Casey et al. 2017
Modelled exposure
Census block groups, contiguous United States

Among urban census block groups, estimated median nighttime noise levels were 46.9 dBA in areas where half the population lived below the poverty line, compared with 44.0 dBA in areas where none did.

These were modelled neighbourhood-level exposures rather than measurements taken inside individual bedrooms. The findings do not establish that everyone living in a lower-income neighbourhood experiences more noise than everyone living in a higher-income one.

They do, however, indicate that environmental noise has a socioeconomic geography.

More recent research has brought the investigation inside the bedroom.

A 2025 study involving 54 adults examined bedroom sound levels alongside polysomnographic recordings. Higher average nighttime sound levels were associated with less REM sleep. The researchers also observed differences in sound exposure between Black and White participants.5

5 Chapagai et al. 2025
Observational
54 adults, one night of in-home polysomnography

Because this was observational research, it cannot establish that noise caused the differences in sleep architecture or fully explains racial disparities in sleep.

Taken together, however, these studies illuminate different parts of the same problem. One identifies inequalities in environmental exposure. Another examines how sound within the bedroom relates to the composition of sleep.

The laboratory findings offer evidence of a possible causal mechanism under controlled conditions.

The studies differ in their populations, methods and measures. Their findings should not be treated as interchangeable. But collectively, they raise an important question about the relationship between place, environment and the biological experience of rest.

Section 5The economics of sleep: lessons from Chennai

One of the most interesting experiments on sleep was not conducted by neuroscientists in a sleep laboratory.

It was conducted by economists in Chennai, India.

In a study published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics in 2021, researchers investigated sleep among low-income urban adults and examined whether increasing sleep could improve economic and psychological outcomes.6

6 Bessone et al. 2021
Randomised field experiment
Low-income urban adults, Chennai, India

Participants spent approximately eight hours in bed each night.

Yet objective measurements using wrist-worn activity monitors indicated that they slept for an average of only five and a half hours.

Their sleep was frequently interrupted. The environments in which participants slept presented challenges involving temperature, noise, physical comfort and other living conditions.

The researchers introduced interventions that included information, encouragement and improvements to home sleeping arrangements. Over three weeks, nighttime sleep increased by approximately 27 minutes, primarily because participants spent more time in bed.

What happened next was unexpected.

The additional nighttime sleep produced no detectable improvements across several measured outcomes, including cognition, productivity, decision-making and well-being. It also came at a cost: participants spent somewhat less time working.

This does not establish that sleeping longer has no benefit. It demonstrates that increasing nighttime sleep through these particular interventions, in this particular population and over the study period, did not produce the improvements researchers anticipated.

But the experiment had another component.

Some participants were given opportunities to take afternoon naps at their workplace.

Unlike the nighttime intervention, the nap intervention improved a combined measure of outcomes, with positive effects on productivity, psychological well-being and cognition. It also reduced the amount of time available for work.

The two interventions differed in more than duration. They took place at different times of day, in different environments and under different circumstances. The study cannot isolate sleep quality as the sole explanation for their contrasting results.

Nevertheless, it challenges an overly simplistic understanding of sleep as an accumulation of interchangeable minutes.

It also illustrates the opportunity cost of rest.

For someone whose income depends directly on time spent working, an additional hour in bed may come at the expense of earnings. The benefits of sleep may be uncertain, delayed or difficult to observe, while the financial cost of foregoing work can be immediate.

From this perspective, insufficient sleep cannot always be understood as a failure to recognise its importance.

It may reflect a constrained allocation of time among competing necessities.

Section 6What does it cost to sleep well?

Across these different areas of research, five resources emerge as a useful way of thinking about the opportunity to sleep: time, space, quiet, physical comfort and security.

This is not a validated scientific classification. It is an editorial framework for considering how social and economic circumstances shape sleeping environments.

TimeSpaceQuietPhysical comfortSecurity

Time

Time is perhaps the most obvious prerequisite for sleep. Yet control over one’s time is not distributed equally.

Consider workers whose schedules change with little notice, employees required to remain available outside conventional working hours, people working multiple jobs and those balancing employment with unpaid caregiving.

In each case, the opportunity to sleep may depend on decisions made by someone else.

Research suggests that changing these arrangements can influence sleep.

In 2017, Seattle introduced a Secure Scheduling ordinance intended to improve the predictability of working hours for covered employees.

Researchers subsequently examined the policy using a natural experiment, comparing changes among workers in Seattle with those in other cities. Greater schedule predictability was accompanied by improvements in self-reported sleep quality, subjective well-being and economic security.7

7 Harknett, Schneider and Irwin 2021
Natural experiment
Workers in Seattle compared with other cities

A separate randomised workplace intervention, published in 2015, increased employees’ control over their working time and encouraged managers to be more supportive of family responsibilities.

After 12 months, employees receiving the intervention slept approximately eight minutes longer per day than those in the control group, as measured through actigraphy. They also reported greater sleep sufficiency, although certain other sleep measures did not differ between groups.8

8 Olson et al. 2015
Randomised workplace trial
Employees followed for 12 months, actigraphy

Neither intervention involved prescribing a sound, changing a mattress or introducing a new bedtime ritual.

Both altered aspects of the organisation of work.

Unpaid work matters, too. A systematic review of 22 studies examining informal family caregivers of ill adults documented substantial sleep difficulties, with outcomes influenced by characteristics of both caregivers and the people receiving care.9

9 Byun et al. 2016
Systematic review
22 studies of family caregivers of ill adults

Caregiving is essential to the functioning of society. Its demands, including those occurring throughout the night, are not always visible in conventional discussions of working hours or economic productivity.

Space

The bedroom is often presented as a private, controllable environment.

For many people, it is neither.

A room may serve simultaneously as a bedroom, a living area, a workspace and a space shared by multiple generations. Its acoustic and lighting conditions may depend on the needs and schedules of several occupants.

Even where a household has a separate bedroom, its occupants may have little authority to modify the building itself.

Housing affordability therefore influences more than the size or location of a home. It can influence the degree of control people have over their sensory environment.

Privacy, physical separation and the ability to establish consistent sleeping conditions are resources that housing can provide.

They are also resources that housing constraints can restrict.

Quiet

The studies discussed earlier suggest that the distribution of environmental noise deserves attention as a potential contributor to disparities in sleep.

But exposure is only part of the equation.

Two homes beside the same road can have different indoor sound levels because of their windows, construction materials, orientation and ventilation.

A person may purchase insulation, move to a quieter neighbourhood or choose a bedroom facing away from the street. Another person may lack those options.

Quiet is not exclusively a feature of geography. It can also be something that money allows us to create.

Physical comfort

Temperature reveals another dimension of the relationship between sleep and economic resources.

In 2022, researchers linked more than seven million sleep records from 47,628 people across 68 countries with local weather data. Warmer nighttime temperatures were associated with reduced sleep duration, with evidence suggesting greater vulnerability among certain demographic groups and residents of lower-income countries.10

10 Minor et al. 2022
Observational, global
7 million+ sleep records, 47,628 people, 68 countries

The participants were users of wearable sleep-tracking devices, rather than a representative sample of the global population. The study examined outdoor temperature data and estimated sleep outcomes; it did not directly measure every participant’s bedroom temperature or access to cooling.

Even with these limitations, it raises questions about the distribution of our capacity to adapt to a warming world.

Air conditioning, ventilation, building insulation and reliable electricity can influence the temperatures people experience indoors. Access to these resources depends on infrastructure, housing and affordability.

Temperature can also interact with sound.

For a person living near a busy road, closing a window may reduce noise while increasing heat. Opening it may improve ventilation while exposing the bedroom to traffic.

Neither choice necessarily produces a comfortable sleeping environment.

The relevant constraint is not simply whether a window is open or closed. It is whether the occupant has another viable option.

Security

Sleep involves relinquishing a degree of conscious awareness of one’s surroundings.

The ability to do so comfortably may be affected by physical safety, financial uncertainty and the stability of one’s living arrangements.

In 2024, researchers analysing longitudinal data from more than 11,000 individuals in the United Kingdom found that difficulties meeting housing payments were associated with a higher probability of experiencing sleep disturbance due to worry.11

11 Mason et al. 2024
Longitudinal panel
11,164 individuals, United Kingdom, 2009 to 2019

This was a specific measure of self-reported sleep disturbance, not a laboratory assessment of sleep architecture. Nonetheless, the findings illustrate how circumstances extending beyond the physical bedroom can intrude upon the experience of sleep.

A quiet room can still be occupied by someone worried about whether they will be able to afford it next month.

Section 7Sleep as a social determinant, and a socially determined outcome

A 2023 narrative synthesis examining 336 studies investigated the relationship between socioeconomic status and sleep health.12

12 Papadopoulos and Etindele Sosso 2023
Narrative synthesis
336 studies across three decades

The researchers found relatively consistent evidence linking socioeconomic disadvantage with several aspects of poorer sleep, including disturbed sleep, reduced sleep continuity and lower sleep satisfaction.

Associations with sleep duration were less consistent.

That distinction is important.

If socioeconomic inequality in sleep manifests substantially through disrupted, irregular or unsatisfactory sleep, a narrow emphasis on duration may obscure meaningful differences between populations.

It is also possible that the relationship operates in both directions.

Socioeconomic circumstances can constrain sleep. Poor sleep, in turn, may affect aspects of health, cognition and daily functioning that influence economic opportunities.

However, the magnitude and direction of these relationships depend on the population, outcome and circumstances being studied. As the Chennai experiment illustrates, improving one measure of sleep does not automatically translate into improved economic outcomes.

A self-reinforcing relationship between disadvantage and inadequate sleep is therefore a plausible concern, not a universally established causal cycle.

The broader implication is that sleep deserves attention both as an influence on human well-being and as an outcome shaped by the distribution of social and economic resources.

Section 8The politics of sleep

The politics of sleep need not be understood in partisan terms.

Politics, in this context, concerns the collective decisions that determine who is exposed to particular conditions, who benefits from them and who bears their costs.

Consider a city that operates throughout the night.

Late-night transport allows people and goods to move. Airports connect economies. Construction creates infrastructure. Hospitals provide essential care. Restaurants, warehouses and other businesses serve demands that extend beyond conventional working hours.

These activities create genuine social and economic value.

They can also generate noise, disrupt schedules and redistribute opportunities for rest.

The benefits and costs are not necessarily experienced by the same people.

A consumer may benefit from an overnight delivery without encountering the working conditions of the person facilitating it. A traveller may benefit from an early-morning flight while residents near the airport experience its acoustic consequences.

Economists describe costs imposed on others that are not fully reflected in the price of an activity as negative externalities.

Environmental noise is one example of how such costs can enter the sleeping environment.

The question is not whether cities should cease to function at night. It is how the benefits of a continuously operating economy can be balanced against the consequences for those whose rest it may disrupt.

The World Health Organization’s environmental noise guidelines recognise noise exposure as a public health concern and provide recommendations covering sources including road, rail and aircraft traffic.13

13 WHO Regional Office for Europe 2018
Guidelines
Road, rail and aircraft noise, among other sources

This places sleep-related environmental conditions within the remit of public institutions, rather than treating them exclusively as matters of personal preference.

Housing standards, transport planning, construction regulations, working-time policies, energy infrastructure and support for caregivers all influence some of the conditions in which people sleep.

These decisions involve trade-offs. Acoustic insulation costs money. Restrictions on nighttime activity can have economic consequences. More predictable working arrangements may affect operational flexibility. Cooling requires energy, with its own environmental implications.

There is no single intervention that resolves every dimension of sleep inequality.

But recognising these trade-offs is different from ignoring them.

Section 9Beyond the bedroom

The research discussed here operates at several different scales.

A laboratory experiment examines what happens when sound reaches a sleeping person.

A neighbourhood study investigates how environmental noise is distributed.

An economics experiment explores what happens when people are given additional opportunities to sleep.

A workplace intervention changes the organisation of time.

A housing study examines how financial insecurity accompanies disturbances in rest.

Together, these perspectives suggest that sleep cannot be fully understood within the boundaries of the bedroom.

There are circumstances in which individual interventions are practical and valuable. There are others in which changing the environment at its source may offer a more appropriate response.

A pair of earplugs operates at the level of an individual. Acoustic standards operate at the level of a building. Transport planning operates at the level of a neighbourhood or city.

All address different aspects of the relationship between sound and sleep.

The challenge is to determine which interventions are effective, for whom, under what conditions, and at what cost.

That requires more research in real-world environments, particularly among populations whose sleeping conditions differ substantially from those in controlled laboratory studies.

It also requires measuring more than the number of hours people sleep.

Section 10Is sleep a luxury?

Strictly speaking, sleep is not a luxury. It is a biological necessity.

But the resources that enable uninterrupted, comfortable and sufficient rest can resemble luxuries in the way they are distributed.

The ability to control the temperature of a room. To choose when to go to bed. To close a window without becoming uncomfortable. To live away from persistent environmental noise. To have a room of one’s own. To rest without immediate financial consequences.

These are not merely characteristics of personal sleep routines.

They reflect the organisation of housing, labour, infrastructure, households and economies.

Much of the contemporary conversation about sleep asks what individuals can do differently.

Perhaps an equally important question is what would need to be different about the world in which they sleep.

Because while the need for sleep is universal, the opportunity to sleep well is not equally available.

About this essay

This essay is part of The Science of Sound, an independent Hush editorial project exploring how sound interacts with human perception, physiology, behaviour and society.

It draws on selected published research across sleep science, environmental health, economics and social policy. It is a research-informed editorial essay, not a systematic literature review, original empirical study or source of personalised medical guidance.

Experimental findings, observational associations and editorial interpretations should be understood according to their respective methodological limitations.

Figures are drawn by Hush from the numbers reported in each source. Schematics are marked as such and contain no data.
