Can sound really help you sleep?

Short answer: it depends on the sound and on what you mean by "help". Steady noise may get some people to sleep a little sooner. All-night pink noise may change the mix of sleep stages. Music has the best evidence, but for how well people feel they slept. And for 432 Hz, 528 Hz and binaural beats, the research is not there yet.

By Hush Focus Music. Published 24 November 2026. Research notes for the episode below; every number comes from the paper it is attributed to.

The four sources, one by one

1. Steady noise and falling asleep: the 2017 sleep laboratory experiment

Eighteen healthy adults each spent two nights in a sleep laboratory, about a week apart. To make falling asleep harder, they were sent to bed 90 minutes before their usual bedtime. One night had normal background sound (about 40 dB); the other had broadband noise from two speakers (about 46 dB), in random order.

Result: the median time to reach stage 2 sleep was 19 minutes without the noise and 13 minutes with it, a reduction of about 38 percent.

Limits: 18 people, one night per condition, healthy volunteers, and an artificial "early bedtime" model of a bad night.

Messineo L, Taranto-Montemurro L, Sands SA, Oliveira Marques MD, Azabarzin A, Wellman DA. Broadband Sound Administration Improves Sleep Onset Latency in Healthy Subjects in a Model of Transient Insomnia. Frontiers in Neurology, 2017. doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2017.00718

2. Steady noise overall: the 2021 systematic review

Researchers gathered every study they could find on continuous white or similar broadband noise and sleep: 38 studies. The studies differed widely in the kind of noise, how sleep was measured and what the comparison was, and their findings ranged from noise improving sleep to noise disturbing it.

Result: using the GRADE criteria, the quality of evidence that continuous noise improves sleep was rated very low.

Limits: a review is only as good as the studies in it, and that is the finding here.

Riedy SM, Smith MG, Rocha S, Basner M. Noise as a sleep aid: A systematic review. Sleep Medicine Reviews, 2021. doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2020.101385

3. Pink noise all night: the 2026 sleep laboratory study

Twenty-five healthy adults (average age 28.5) spent seven nights in a sleep laboratory under different sound conditions, with their sleep recorded throughout. One condition was continuous pink noise at 50 dB, roughly the level of moderate rain.

Compared with the quiet nightChange with pink noise at 50 dB
Total sleep time+0.6 minutes (not significant)
Deep sleep (N3)−4.5 minutes (not significant)
REM sleep−18.6 minutes
Light sleep (N2)+20.1 minutes

In other words, people slept just as long, but REM sleep was largely exchanged for light sleep.

Limits: one study, healthy young adults who did not normally sleep with noise, one week, and the REM result is at 50 dB. Quieter levels and people with sleep difficulties were not what this result describes.

Basner M, et al. Efficacy of pink noise and earplugs for mitigating the effects of intermittent environmental noise exposure on sleep. Sleep, 2026. doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsag001

4. Music: the 2022 Cochrane review

The review included 13 trials with 1,007 adults who had sleep difficulties. People listened to recorded music for 25 to 60 minutes a day, for between three days and three months, and were compared with no treatment or usual care.

Result: in the 10 trials (708 people) that measured it, self-rated sleep quality improved by about 2.8 points on the 21-point Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, rated moderate certainty. No trial reported any adverse effects.

Limits: participants knew they were listening to music, so expectation may play a part. The three trials (136 people) that measured sleep objectively did not show a clear improvement.

Jespersen KV, Pando-Naude V, Koenig J, Jennum P, Vuust P. Listening to music for insomnia in adults. Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2022. doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD010459.pub3

What the episode could not fit

  1. The 2017 noise experiment measured one night per condition in 18 people.
  2. The pink noise study tested healthy sleepers at 50 dB. Quieter levels and people who struggle to sleep are a different question.
  3. The music review found that objective sleep measures did not clearly improve, only how people rated their sleep.
  4. Falling asleep faster and sleeping better are separate outcomes, and no study we found weighs one against the other for people who lie awake.

Try it properly

One good or bad night tells you very little. If you want to test a sound on yourself, keep it simple and consistent: the same sound at the same low volume for seven nights, then seven nights without. Each morning, note roughly how long it took to fall asleep and how rested you feel out of five. Compare the two weeks, not single nights.

Questions people ask

Does white noise help you sleep?

It may help some people fall asleep sooner. In one sleep laboratory experiment with 18 healthy adults, broadband noise cut the median time to reach stage 2 sleep from 19 to 13 minutes. A systematic review of 38 studies rated the overall evidence as very low quality, because the studies were small, varied and often inconsistent.

Does pink noise reduce REM sleep?

In one 2026 sleep laboratory study of 25 healthy adults, pink noise played all night at 50 dB went with about 19 minutes less REM sleep and about 20 minutes more light (N2) sleep. Total sleep time and deep sleep did not change significantly. It is one study, at one volume, in healthy sleepers.

Does music help you sleep?

Music has the most encouraging evidence, for how well people feel they slept. A 2022 Cochrane review found moderate-certainty evidence, from 10 trials and 708 adults with sleep difficulties, that listening to music improved self-rated sleep quality. Three studies that measured sleep objectively did not show a clear improvement.

Do 432 Hz, 528 Hz or binaural beats help you sleep?

There is not enough research to say. For sleep specifically, we found only a handful of small studies and no clear answer. That is the subject of the next episode.

Is it better to fall asleep faster or to sleep better?

They are not the same thing, and nobody appears to have measured the trade-off for people who struggle to fall asleep. The noise studies above were done in healthy sleepers.

How we made this

We read the four papers, not the headlines about them. Each claim in the episode is tied to one source, shown with its size and its limits. Where the evidence is weak, we say so. This page describes published research for general interest and is not medical advice.
